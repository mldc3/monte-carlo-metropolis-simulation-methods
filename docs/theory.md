# Theory background

## 1. Monte Carlo methods in computational physics

Monte Carlo methods are numerical techniques that use random or pseudo-random sampling to estimate quantities that may be difficult to compute deterministically. In physics, they are useful because many relevant objects are naturally high-dimensional: phase-space averages, configuration sums, transport processes, radiation paths, molecular conformations and statistical-mechanics observables.

The core idea is probabilistic. Instead of evaluating every possible configuration, we draw representative samples and use the law of large numbers to estimate the target quantity. If the samples are independent and correctly distributed, the estimator converges to the expected value. The main limitation is that the statistical error typically decreases as

```math
\sigma \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}},
```

where `N` is the number of samples. This convergence is slow compared with many deterministic quadrature methods in low-dimensional problems, but Monte Carlo becomes attractive in high-dimensional spaces where deterministic grids become exponentially expensive.

## 2. Pseudo-random numbers and reproducibility

In computational work, random numbers are usually pseudo-random: they are generated by deterministic algorithms from an initial seed. This is not a weakness for scientific computing. On the contrary, seeding the generator makes simulations reproducible, which is essential for debugging, validation and comparison between numerical experiments.

In the scripts, `numpy.random.default_rng(seed)` is used to control the random-number generator. This makes the plots reproducible while preserving the stochastic nature of the method.

## 3. Rejection Monte Carlo integration

Consider a non-negative function `f(x)` on an interval `[a, b]`. Suppose we know a constant `M` such that

```math
0 \leq f(x) \leq M \qquad \forall x \in [a,b].
```

The integral

```math
I = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx
```

is the area under the curve. Rejection Monte Carlo estimates this area by sampling points uniformly in the rectangle `[a,b] x [0,M]`.

For each random point `(x_i, y_i)`, define an indicator variable

```math
X_i = \begin{cases}
1, & y_i \leq f(x_i),\\
0, & y_i > f(x_i).
\end{cases}
```

If `k` points fall below the curve, the fraction `k/N` estimates the probability that a uniformly sampled point lies in the area under the curve. Because the rectangle area is

```math
A = (b-a)M,
```

the integral estimator is

```math
\hat I = A\frac{k}{N} = (b-a)M\frac{k}{N}.
```

This estimator is unbiased if `M` is at least the true maximum of the function. If `M` is chosen too low, part of the function lies outside the sampling rectangle and the estimator converges to a truncated integral rather than the correct value. If `M` is too high, the estimator remains valid but inefficient: many points are rejected, the acceptance probability decreases and more samples are needed for the same precision.

## 4. Error scaling

The accepted/rejected variable is Bernoulli distributed with probability

```math
p = \frac{I}{A}.
```

The number of accepted points follows a binomial distribution:

```math
k \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(N,p).
```

The variance of the estimator is therefore

```math
\mathrm{Var}(\hat I)
= A^2\frac{p(1-p)}{N}.
```

Thus the standard deviation scales as `1/sqrt(N)`. This is the central diagnostic plotted in the repository: increasing the sample size reduces fluctuations, but the improvement is gradual.

## 5. Hypersphere volume by Monte Carlo

A second example is the volume of a `d`-dimensional ball of radius `R`, defined by

```math
x_1^2+x_2^2+\cdots+x_d^2 \leq R^2.
```

The exact volume is

```math
V_d(R) = \frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2+1)}R^d.
```

To estimate it numerically, sample points uniformly from the containing hypercube `[-R,R]^d`. The hypercube volume is

```math
V_{cube} = (2R)^d.
```

If `N_inside` out of `N` points lie inside the hypersphere, the Monte Carlo estimator is

```math
\hat V_d = (2R)^d\frac{N_{inside}}{N}.
```

This problem illustrates an important high-dimensional effect. As dimension grows, the ratio between the hypersphere volume and the containing hypercube volume can become small. That means most sampled points fall outside the hypersphere, so the estimator needs many more samples to maintain a small relative error.

## 6. Metropolis Monte Carlo

The Metropolis algorithm is designed to sample configurations according to a target distribution without having to enumerate all possible states. In statistical mechanics, the desired equilibrium probability of a configuration `R` with energy `E(R)` in the canonical ensemble is proportional to the Boltzmann weight:

```math
P(R) \propto e^{-E(R)/(k_BT)}.
```

The partition function normalizes this distribution, but direct evaluation is often impossible because the number of configurations is enormous. The Metropolis method avoids this problem by generating a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is the Boltzmann distribution.

At each step:

1. choose a current configuration `i`;
2. propose a new configuration `j`;
3. compute the energy difference

```math
\Delta E = E_j - E_i;
```

4. accept the move with probability

```math
A(i\to j) = \min\left(1, e^{-\Delta E/(k_BT)}\right).
```

If the proposed state has lower energy, it is always accepted. If it has higher energy, it can still be accepted with a probability controlled by the temperature. This allows the system to explore configuration space while still favouring low-energy states.

## 7. Model: particles in a cubic quantum box

In the Metropolis script, each particle is represented by three positive quantum numbers `(n_x, n_y, n_z)`. In reduced units, the energy of a particle in a cubic box of length `L` is

```math
E(n_x,n_y,n_z) = \frac{h^2\pi^2}{2mL^2}(n_x^2+n_y^2+n_z^2).
```

The simulation begins with all particles in the ground state `(1,1,1)`. A Metropolis proposal selects one particle, one direction and changes the corresponding quantum number by `+1` or `-1`, rejecting any proposal that would make a quantum number smaller than one. The resulting energy change determines whether the move is accepted.

This model is simple, but it captures several important ideas:

- the role of Boltzmann acceptance;
- thermal exploration of energy levels;
- equilibration from a low-energy initial state;
- dependence of energy on temperature, particle number and box size;
- fluctuations around equilibrium rather than convergence to a single deterministic state.

## 8. Connection with molecular simulation

The methods in this repository are directly connected with molecular modelling. In atomistic simulation, one often needs to explore an energy landscape, estimate ensemble averages and test convergence. Monte Carlo methods sample configurations stochastically, while molecular dynamics follows trajectories by integrating Newton's equations.

In molecular dynamics with Lennard-Jones particles, the pair potential is often written as

```math
U(r) = 4\epsilon\left[\left(\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{12} - \left(\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)^6\right].
```

The steep repulsive term prevents overlap, and the attractive term approximates dispersion interactions. For finite systems intended to represent bulk materials, periodic boundary conditions and the minimum-image convention reduce surface effects. The velocity-Verlet method is commonly used to integrate the equations of motion because it is explicit, time-reversible and has good long-time energy behaviour for conservative systems.

Therefore, although the scripts here are compact coursework examples, they demonstrate the same numerical thinking used in larger scientific codes: sampling, acceptance criteria, validation, convergence analysis, energy diagnostics and careful interpretation of stochastic results.
